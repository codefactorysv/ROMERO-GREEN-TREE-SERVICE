# Off-page SEO and backlinks strategy

Backlinks, citations and reviews are the part of SEO that does not live in this
repository. This is the plan for it.

Read alongside the **Pending business info** section of the [README](../README.md):
several of the highest-impact items below are blocked on data the business has not
confirmed yet, and none of that data may be guessed.

Off-page SEO is everything that happens away from `romerogreentree.com` that makes search
engines and customers trust the business: the Google Business Profile, consistent listings
across the web, reviews, and links earned from real sites.

For a local service business, off-page work is not a nice-to-have that comes after the site is
"finished." The site is finished. The map pack  -  the three-business block above the organic
results  -  is where most "tree service near me" clicks go, and nothing on the site can win that
block by itself. That is what this document is for.

One rule governs the whole plan: **every listing, every profile, every claim must be true and
confirmed.** A listing built on a guessed address or an invented service area is worse than no
listing, because bad citation data spreads to other platforms and is slow and tedious to unwind.

---

## Blocked until the client confirms

The README's "Pending business info" section is not a formality. Three of the highest-impact
off-page items are hard-blocked on it. Nothing below invents a value to get past a block.

| Missing information | What it blocks | Severity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Business address** (a real street address, even if never shown publicly) | Google Business Profile verification, Bing Places, Apple Business Connect, the "A" in every citation, the `address` field in the site's schema | **Critical  -  blocks the #1 item** |
| **Service area** (the cities / counties actually served) | Service-area-business setup in GBP, `areaServed` in schema, all location-targeted content, which chamber of commerce to join, which local media to pitch | **Critical** |
| **Regular business hours** (office hours, distinct from 24/7 emergency) | The hours field on every profile. Only the 24/7 **emergency** availability is confirmed, and it must never be presented as office hours | High |
| **Social media profiles** (do any exist, and who controls them?) | Facebook / Instagram / Nextdoor claiming, the `sameAs` array in schema, review funnels beyond Google | High |
| **Certificate of insurance (COI)** | Several directories request proof before displaying an "insured" badge. The site says *insured* and must never say *licensed*, *certified*, or *ISA certified arborist* | Medium |
| **Business entity name and formation details** (DBA vs. LLC, EIN) | Chamber of commerce and trade association applications; a BBB listing if one is ever pursued | Medium |
| **Whether anyone on the crew holds a credential** | ISA "Find an Arborist" and every credential-gated directory. Until confirmed in writing, assume **no credential** and claim none | Medium |
| **Customers willing to leave a review** | The entire review program. The site currently makes **zero** review claims  -  that is correct and must stay that way until real reviews exist | High |

**What is NOT blocked:** deciding the canonical NAP format, drafting the GBP content
(description, services, photo set, Q&A), preparing the review request scripts, translating the
blog into Spanish, and building the outreach target list. All of that can be finished now, so
that the day the address lands the profile goes live in an hour instead of a week.

An ordered shortlist. Items 1-4 need no client input at all.

1. **Lock the canonical NAP format** and write it into one document everyone copies from.
   Fifteen minutes now prevents months of inconsistent citations. → [NAP Consistency](#2-nap-consistency)
2. **Sort and caption the photo library.** The client already has real job photos in
   `public/images/real/`. Group them into before/after pairs, equipment, crew-at-work, and
   finished-job sets so GBP photos are ready to upload the moment the profile exists.
3. **Draft the GBP business description, services list, and 8-10 Q&A entries** against confirmed
   facts only. Park them in a document.
4. **Ask the client the eight blocking questions in one message, not eight.** Address, service
   area, office hours, social accounts, entity name, COI, credentials, and a list of recent

6. **Build the tier-one citations**  -  Bing Places, Apple Business Connect, Yelp, Nextdoor,

7. **Start the review program** with the three to five most recent satisfied customers.
   → [Review Strategy](#6-review-strategy)

Everything after that is compounding, lower-urgency work.

## 1. Google Business Profile

**Effort: Medium · Impact: Very High**

### What it is

Google Business Profile (GBP, formerly Google My Business) is the free listing that puts a
business in Google Maps and in the local map pack. It is managed at
[business.google.com](https://business.google.com), and it is without close competition the
single highest-leverage off-page asset a local tree service can own.

### Why it matters

Someone with a limb through their roof searches on a phone and taps a result from the map pack.
They do not scroll to organic result four. For emergency-driven, proximity-driven demand  -  which
is exactly what tree service is  -  the profile *is* the storefront. The website's job is largely
to convert the traffic the profile sends it.

### Claiming and verification  -  the blocker

| Step | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Create / claim the profile | Ready  -  needs the owning Google account decided |
| Choose the business type | **BLOCKED**  -  depends on whether there is a location customers visit |
| Verify | **BLOCKED**  -  verification requires a real address |

Google offers two shapes for a business like this:

- **Storefront**  -  customers come to a location. Almost certainly not the case here.
- **Service-area business (SAB)**  -  the address is entered during setup but **hidden from the
  public listing**, and the business instead declares the cities, counties, or ZIP regions it
  serves.

SAB is the right shape for a tree service, and one point deserves saying plainly: **the address
is still required even though it is hidden.** Google needs a verifiable location. A home address
is acceptable and common for service-area businesses; it simply is not displayed.

Verification today is usually **video verification**  -  a single unbroken recording showing the
location, the tools and equipment of the trade (climbing gear, saws, chipper, grinder, truck),
and evidence that the person recording manages the business. For a tree service this is an
advantage: the equipment is unmistakable and hard to fake, which is exactly what the review is
looking for. Stage the gear before recording. Postcard, phone, and email verification still
appear for some profiles, but plan for video.

> **Do not** enter a guessed address, a mailbox store, or a virtual office. Google suspends
> profiles for exactly this, and reinstating a suspended profile is far harder than creating a
> clean one. Wait for the real address.

### Categories

The primary category carries more ranking weight than any other single field on the profile.

- **Primary: `Tree service`.** This is the correct primary and should not be traded for anything
  broader like "Landscaper." A broad primary dilutes relevance for the emergency and removal
  queries that actually convert.
- **Secondary categories:** add only ones describing work genuinely performed. Likely candidates
  from Google's list include `Landscaper`, `Lawn care service`, and `Landscape designer`. Google's
  category list changes and is regionalized, so verify each one in the live picker rather than
  trusting any written list  -  including this one. Three to five secondaries at most; padding the
  list with loosely related categories weakens the primary signal.

The confirmed services map cleanly onto these: Tree Removal, Tree Pruning, Tree Trimming, and
Limb Cleaning under the primary; Landscaping, Mulching, Flower Beds, and Plant Grass under the
landscaping secondaries; Stump Grinding under either.

### Services list

Below categories, GBP allows a custom services list with short descriptions. Enter all nine
confirmed services as individual entries:

Tree Removal · Tree Pruning · Tree Trimming · Stump Grinding · Landscaping · Limb Cleaning ·
Mulching · Flower Beds · Plant Grass

Add one more for **24/7 Emergency Tree Service**, since it is a confirmed offering and the
highest-intent thing the business does. Each description should be two or three plain sentences.
No prices, no response times, no guarantees, no service-area names.

**Effort: Low · Impact: Medium**

### Photos

This is where the profile is already ahead. The client has genuine job photography in
`public/images/real/`  -  climbers roped into pine canopies, a removal within feet of a house,
storm cleanup at a pond edge. Stock photography is obvious to customers and adds nothing; real
work photos are a differentiator.

| Photo type | What to upload | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logo | The transparent PNG lockup | Square crop |
| Cover | The strongest single action shot | Sets the profile's first impression |
| At work | Climbers, rigging, cutting, chipping | The most persuasive category |
| Equipment | Trucks, chipper, grinder, gear | Also supports video verification |
| Before / after | Paired shots of the same property | Highest engagement of any pair |
| Team | Crew on site, in gear | Builds trust  -  get consent first |

Practical rules: upload steadily rather than dumping everything at once, keep adding new photos
monthly so the profile shows activity, and shoot before/after from the same spot at the same
focal length so the pair reads as one job. Never caption a photo with a city, a customer name,
an identifiable house number, or a price, and get the property owner's permission before posting
a recognizable house.

**Effort: Low (the photos already exist) · Impact: High**

### Q&A

The Q&A section is public and anyone can answer  -  including competitors and people guessing
wrong. Seed it. A business is allowed to post its own questions and answer them, and that is the
only way to control what sits there.

Ten questions worth seeding, each answerable purely from confirmed facts:

1. Do you offer emergency tree service?  -  Yes, 24/7 for emergencies.
2. Are you insured?  -  Yes, insured. (Never "licensed." Never "certified.")
3. Do you charge for an estimate?  -  No, estimates are free.
4. Do you speak Spanish?  -  Yes. Hablamos Español.
5. Do you work on commercial properties?  -  Yes, residential and commercial.
6. Do you grind the stump after a removal?  -  Yes, stump grinding is offered.
7. What is the difference between trimming and pruning?  -  Link the pruning article.
8. Do you haul away the debris?  -  Describe limb cleaning and mulching.
9. Can you help after a storm?  -  Link the emergency article.
10. How do I reach you?  -  (832) 272-4373 or greentreeromero@gmail.com.

Set a monthly reminder to check for new questions from the public. An unanswered or wrongly
answered question sits on the profile indefinitely.

**Effort: Low · Impact: Medium**

### Google Posts

Posts are short updates that appear on the profile. They are widely understood to drop out of
the main view after roughly a week, so they reward cadence over polish.

A realistic calendar built on what actually exists:

- **Weekly:** one job photo with two sentences of context ("Sectional removal of a leaning pine
  over a roofline"). No address, no customer name.
- **Seasonally:** hurricane-season prep in late spring, dormant-season pruning in winter, oak
  wilt timing in spring. Each links the matching blog article.
- **After a storm:** the emergency article plus the phone number. This is the highest-value post
  the business will ever publish, and it is time-sensitive  -  publish while the wind is still in
  the news.
- **Bilingual:** alternate or duplicate posts in Spanish. Very few competitors do this.

Posts must not contain prices, discounts, response-time promises, or review claims.

**Effort: Low, recurring · Impact: Medium**

### How the profile connects to the site's schema

`src/app/layout.tsx` emits a `ProfessionalService` JSON-LD node with a stable `@id`
(`{site}/#organization`) carrying name, telephone, email, url, image, logo, `knowsLanguage` for
English and Spanish, and `makesOffer` entries for each service. The codebase is already built for
the unblocking: `src/lib/content.ts` exports a `businessLocation` object with `enabled: false` and
empty `streetAddress`, `addressLocality`, `addressRegion`, `postalCode`, `areaServed`,
`serviceRadiusKm`, and `openingHours` fields, and `localBusinessSchema()` in `src/lib/seo.ts`
returns an empty object until that flag flips  -  then emits `address`, `geo`, `areaServed` (as
named `AdministrativeArea` entries, or a `GeoCircle` if only a radius is known) and `openingHours`.

Schema and GBP are separate systems  -  schema markup does not create or feed the profile  -  but
Google reconciles the entities it finds for a business, and agreement between them corroborates
while disagreement confuses. So:

1. The `name` in schema, the profile name, and every citation must be byte-identical.
2. `telephone` must match the profile's phone digit for digit.
3. `url` must be the exact canonical the profile's website field points at  -  same protocol, same
   host, same trailing-slash convention.
4. When the address and service area are confirmed, fill in `businessLocation`, set
   `enabled: true`, and make every value match the profile exactly. The README's "After going
   live" section already flags this step.
5. Only populate `openingHours` with **confirmed office hours**. Do not encode the 24/7 emergency
   availability as `Mo-Su 00:00-23:59`  -  that asserts the office is always open, which is not a
   confirmed fact.
6. When social profiles are confirmed, add a `sameAs` array pointing at them. Not before.
7. **Never add `aggregateRating` or `review` to the site's own schema.** Self-serving review
   markup for the business's own entity is against Google's structured data guidelines, and the
   site has no reviews to describe anyway.

**Effort: Low (the plumbing is already in place) · Impact: Medium**

## 2. NAP Consistency

**Effort: Low to decide, Medium to maintain · Impact: High**

### What it is

**NAP** = **N**ame, **A**ddress, **P**hone. It is the identity triple search engines use to decide
whether "Romero Green Tree Service" on Yelp, on Bing, and on a chamber directory is the same
company or three different ones. Some practitioners write it NAP-W to include the website URL,
which deserves the same discipline.

### Why it matters

Local search engines build a confidence score for a business entity by cross-referencing what the
web says about it. Consistent NAP across many sources raises that confidence. Inconsistent NAP  - 
"Romero Green Tree Service" here, "Romero Green Tree Services LLC" there, `832-272-4373` on one
listing and `(832) 272-4373` with a different suite number on another  -  fragments the entity and
suppresses map-pack visibility. It is also genuinely hard to clean up later, because bad data
replicates outward to platforms that scraped it.

The cheapest moment to get this right is before the first listing exists. That moment is now.

### The canonical format  -  decide this before creating any listing

| Field | Canonical value | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Name** | `Romero Green Tree Service` | Exactly this. Singular "Service." No "LLC", no "Tree Removal & Landscaping", no city suffix, no keyword stuffing. Google's guidelines require the real-world business name, and appended keywords are a common cause of suspension and competitor edit requests. Confirm whether the legal entity name differs  -  if it does, use the real-world trading name on listings and reserve the legal name for applications that specifically ask for it. |
| **Address** | **BLOCKED** | Once known, fix the exact string  -  including whether it is `St` or `Street`, `Ste` or `Suite`, and the ZIP+4  -  then never deviate. For a service-area business it is entered but hidden. |
| **Phone (display)** | `(832) 272-4373` | Parentheses, space, hyphen. This exact string wherever a human reads it. |
| **Phone (link)** | `tel:+18322724373` | E.164 for every `href`. Already correct in `siteConfig.phoneHref`. |
| **Website** | `https://romerogreentree.com` | One protocol, one host, one trailing-slash convention. Pick and never vary. |
| **Email** | `greentreeromero@gmail.com` | The same address everywhere. |
| **Slogan** | `Keeping Families Safe Through Professional Tree Services.` | Verbatim, wherever a tagline field exists. |

Two further rules:

- **One phone number, forever.** Do not put call-tracking numbers on citations. Tracking numbers
  are the single most common cause of NAP fragmentation. If call tracking is ever needed, use
  dynamic number insertion on the website only, never in a listing.
- **Store the canonical block in one place** that the client and anyone helping can copy-paste
  from. `src/lib/content.ts` already is that place for the website; mirror it in a plain text
  note for listings.

### The blocked "A"

Until the address is confirmed, **do not create listings that require one.** It is tempting to
"get started" with a city name or a rough area. Don't. A wrong address entered once will be
scraped, syndicated, and still surfacing two years later. The correct sequence is: confirm the
address → fix the canonical string → create listings, starting with GBP.

A small number of platforms accept a service-area business with no public address at all; those
can proceed earlier if they do not require an address field. Verify before submitting.

## 3. Industry Directories

**Effort: Medium · Impact: Medium**

### Set expectations honestly

Most directory links are `nofollow`, `sponsored`, or JavaScript-wrapped redirects. They pass
little or no ranking signal. Anyone selling "500 directory backlinks" is selling either nothing
or a liability.

Directories are still worth doing, for three real reasons:

1. **Citation consistency**  -  they corroborate the NAP entity for local search.
2. **Referral traffic**  -  people genuinely search Yelp and Angi for contractors, and an emergency
   search on any of them can produce a booked job.
3. **Occasional real links**  -  trade associations and chambers often *do* pass followed links, and
   those are worth more than a hundred generic directory profiles.

Link attributes change without notice. Before assuming a link is followed, check the `rel`
attribute on the live page rather than trusting a table  -  including this one, where attribution is
marked as generally observed, not guaranteed.

### Arborist-specific directories  -  read this carefully

The obvious target is the International Society of Arboriculture's **"Find an Arborist"** locator.
It is real, it is well-regarded, and **it is gated on ISA certification.**

Certification is not confirmed and the site correctly says *insured* only, so this listing is
**not available** and must not be pursued or implied. Do not create a profile, do not use
"arborist" as a credential in any listing, and watch for directory signup forms that pre-tick an
"ISA Certified" checkbox.

If the client ever pursues ISA certification, the locator becomes worth revisiting. That is a
business decision about training and exams, not an SEO task.

The **Tree Care Industry Association (TCIA)** offers company membership with a public locator for
finding tree care companies. Membership is a paid trade association membership, not a credential,
and is worth evaluating  -  but TCIA also runs a separate **Accreditation** program, and membership
must never be described as accreditation. See §5.

### Directories not worth the time

Any directory that charges for a "guaranteed dofollow link," has no visible traffic, exists as one
of dozens of near-identical clones, or requires a reciprocal link. Skip them. See §8.

## 4. Local Citations and Data Aggregators

**Effort: Medium · Impact: Medium-High**

### How the ecosystem actually works

A **citation** is any online mention of the business's NAP, with or without a link. Search engines
treat the volume and  -  much more importantly  -  the *consistency* of citations as evidence that a
business is real and located where it claims.

Citations enter the ecosystem two ways:

- **Direct listings**  -  the business creates the profile itself (GBP, Yelp, Bing).
- **Syndicated data**  -  a small number of data providers supply business records in bulk to many
  downstream sites, apps, GPS units, and directories. One record propagates to dozens of
  destinations without anyone submitting anything.

This is why one wrong address is so expensive: it does not stay in one place.

### The main aggregators and platforms

The aggregator landscape has consolidated considerably, and several once-standard free direct
submission channels have been retired. The practical picture today:

| Source | What it is | Access | Effort | Impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Data Axle** | Long-running US business data compiler whose records feed a wide range of downstream directories and apps. | Largely via managed/paid channels now | Medium | Medium |
| **Localeze** (TransUnion) | Business listing data provider feeding search, navigation, and directory partners. | Paid / via partners | Medium | Medium |
| **Foursquare** | A location data provider as well as a consumer app; its place data reaches numerous third-party apps. | Free listing + paid data services | Low | Medium |
| **Yext** | Not an aggregator  -  a *management* platform that pushes and syncs one record out to a large publisher network. | Paid subscription, ongoing | Low effort, ongoing cost | Medium |

Because free direct submission to the compilers has narrowed, the realistic options are:

1. **Manual, by hand, tier by tier.** Free, slow, fully controlled. Recommended for a business
   this size  -  the tier one, two, and three tables in §3 cover the destinations that matter, and
   doing it manually guarantees the canonical NAP is used every time.
2. **A one-time citation-building service.** **BrightLocal**, **Whitespark**, and **Moz Local** all
   offer citation building and/or listing sync. A one-time build is a reasonable time-saver; an
   indefinite subscription usually is not for a single-location business.

### Cleanup and monitoring

Once listings exist, two recurring jobs:

- **Audit quarterly.** Search `"Romero Green Tree Service"` and `"832-272-4373"` in both phone
  formats and see what comes back. Fix anything that disagrees with the canonical block. Citation
  audit tools from the vendors above automate the searching.
- **Watch for duplicates.** Duplicate GBP listings actively suppress each other and get created by
  accident more often than people expect. Report duplicates through Google's own flow rather than
  deleting one, which can orphan reviews.

**Never** let an onboarding wizard auto-fill hours, service area, or credentials. Its defaults are
guesses, and a guessed "Mon-Fri 9-5" contradicts the confirmed facts.

## 5. Texas-Specific Local Organizations

**Effort: Medium · Impact: Medium-High (chamber links are often followed)**

### Why this tier is disproportionately valuable

Chamber of commerce and trade association member directories are among the few directory-class
links that are frequently **followed**, come from a genuinely local or industry-relevant domain,
and are hard for competitors to replicate at scale. They also produce actual business: commercial
property managers, HOA boards, and small business owners are exactly the audience, and the
confirmed commercial service offering is directly relevant.

### Chambers of commerce  -  a category, not a name

**No city is confirmed, so no specific chamber can be named here.** Naming one would imply a
service area the business has not confirmed. Once the city and service area are known, this is the
process:

1. **Identify the chambers whose territory the business actually operates in.** Search
   `"<city> chamber of commerce"` and `"<county> chamber of commerce"`. Most Texas metros have a
   main regional chamber plus several suburb, district, and neighborhood chambers.
2. **Look for a Hispanic or Latino chamber of commerce in the same area.** These exist in most
   Texas metros and at the state level, are typically smaller and more engaged, and are a natural
   fit given the confirmed bilingual English/Spanish capability. Once the city is known, this is
   one of the highest-value single actions available to this business.
3. **Check the member directory before paying.** Open an existing member's listing and inspect the
   outbound link's `rel` attribute. A followed link from a real local chamber justifies the
   membership on its own; a nofollowed one still delivers citation value and referrals, but price
   it accordingly.
4. **Prefer chambers with an active events calendar and a member-news or blog section.** Those
   produce recurring linkable moments, not just one directory row.
5. **Also look for:** BNI or similar structured referral groups, local business and merchant
   associations, downtown associations, and the local Small Business Development Center (SBDC)  - 
   part of a real national network hosted at Texas universities.
6. **Adjacent-trade referral relationships** are worth more than most directories. Roofers, fence
   contractors, pool builders, landscape architects, property managers, and independent insurance
   agents all encounter tree problems constantly. These relationships produce referrals and
   sometimes genuine partner-page links.

### State and national organizations  -  these can be named

These exist independent of any city, so they can be identified specifically.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Texas Nursery & Landscape Association (TNLA)** | The state trade association for the nursery and landscape industry, with a member directory. | Directly relevant to the landscaping, mulching, flower bed, and plant grass services. | Paid membership | Medium | Medium |
| **Tree Care Industry Association (TCIA)** | National trade association for commercial tree care companies; runs a public company locator, safety programs, and training. | The closest industry-body fit. **Membership ≠ Accreditation**  -  never conflate them. | Paid membership | Medium | Medium |
| **National Association of Landscape Professionals (NALP)** | National trade association for landscape and lawn care companies. | Relevant to the landscaping half of the service list. | Paid membership | Medium | Low-Medium |
| **ISA Texas Chapter** | The Texas chapter of the International Society of Arboriculture. Runs events, training, and education. | Worth following for education and networking. **Any listing benefit is gated on certification**  -  see §3. | Varies | Low | Low (until certified) |
| **Texas Forestry Association** | State association focused on forestry and forest resources in Texas. | Peripheral to residential tree service, but a legitimate Texas-specific organization worth knowing. | Paid membership | Medium | Low |
| **Texas A&M Forest Service** | The state forestry agency. Publishes tree care, storm recovery, and pest guidance and coordinates urban forestry programs. | **Not a directory**  -  an authority to cite in content and a source of genuinely useful public information. Citing it well is a content asset. | Free to reference | Low | Medium (as a content source) |

### Community involvement as a link source

Real, and a specifically good fit for this business:

- **Arbor Day and Tree City USA activities.** The Arbor Day Foundation's Tree City USA program is
  real and many Texas municipalities participate. Cities running tree plantings and Arbor Day
  events routinely thank and link sponsors and volunteer crews. A tree service donating equipment,
  labor, or expertise to a planting is an authentic fit and a genuine link opportunity  -  not a
  purchased placement.
- **Post-storm community cleanup.** After a significant wind event, local coverage of cleanup
  efforts is common. Participation is legitimate community work that sometimes earns a mention.
- **Youth sports, school, and church sponsorships.** Small, cheap, and usually produce a followed
  link from a `.org` or school domain. Modest but real.
- **Vocational and high school programs.** Offering a safety demonstration or equipment talk to a
  horticulture or ag-science program is genuinely useful and frequently written up.

One constraint: any of these produce local coverage naming a city. That is fine  -  a news article
saying where an event happened is the publisher's statement. The **website** still must not claim
a service area until the client confirms one.

## 6. Review Strategy

**Effort: Low per request, High in discipline · Impact: Very High**

Reviews affect map-pack ranking and they affect conversion. A profile with a handful of detailed,
recent, genuine reviews outperforms one with none  -  and outperforms one with a pile of obviously
fake five-star one-liners.

### The site's current position  -  keep it

`src/lib/content.ts` and the rendered site make **zero** review claims. No star ratings, no
testimonial block, no review counts, no `aggregateRating` in the JSON-LD. **This is correct and
must not change until real reviews exist**  -  and even then, self-serving review markup for the
business's own entity is against Google's structured data guidelines. Real reviews live on the
platforms that collected them.

### Who to ask

- Customers whose job went well and who said so out loud. An unprompted thank-you is the best
  signal.
- Emergency customers, once the crisis has passed and the invoice is settled. Gratitude after a
  tree comes off a roof is real, and those are the most persuasive reviews a tree service will
  ever get.
- Commercial and property-management clients, for commercial credibility.
- **Spanish-speaking customers, in Spanish.** Spanish-language reviews on the profile prove the
  bilingual capability in a way no marketing copy can. A genuine differentiator.
- Do **not** ask friends, family, employees, or anyone who was not a customer. This is the most
  common way small businesses get reviews removed and profiles flagged.

### Timing

Ask when the customer is standing in the finished yard at the moment of visible relief  -  the stump
gone, the driveway swept, the limb off the roof. Ask in person first, then follow up once by text
or email within 24 hours with the link. One follow-up. If they do not respond, stop.

### Make it effortless

1. Get the GBP short review link (available in the profile's "Ask for reviews" tool) and shorten it.
2. Put it in a saved text message the crew lead can send from the truck in ten seconds.
3. Add it to the invoice or receipt.
4. Prepare the message in **both English and Spanish**. Half the value of a bilingual crew is lost
   if the follow-up is English-only.
5. Never ask the customer to say anything specific. Prompted wording produces reviews that read
   alike, and reviews that read alike get filtered.

A workable script  -  plain, no pressure:

> "If you were happy with how the job went, a quick Google review really helps a small business
> like ours. Here's the link. Thanks either way  -  and call us any time."

> "Si quedó contento con el trabajo, una reseña en Google nos ayuda mucho. Aquí está el enlace.
> Gracias, y llámenos cuando lo necesite."

### Responding to reviews

Respond to every review, positive and negative, within a few days. Responses are public and are
read by future customers far more than by the original reviewer.

For a negative review: thank them, do not argue, do not disclose job details or the address,
acknowledge the specific concern, say what will be done, and move it offline with the phone
number. One reply. Never a second. A calm reply under an angry review converts better than the
angry review costs; a defensive reply is worse than no reply at all.

If a review is clearly fake or violates platform policy, report it through the platform's flow and
be patient. Do not respond publicly to bait.

### The hard warning

**Do not incentivize reviews. Do not buy reviews. Do not write reviews. Do not gate reviews.**

- Google's policies prohibit incentivized and fake reviews. Violations get reviews stripped and can
  escalate to a manual action or profile suspension.
- **Review gating**  -  asking how the experience was first and sending the review link only to those
  who answer positively  -  is prohibited. It is common, it is enforced against, and it is not a
  clever workaround.
- **Yelp goes further and asks businesses not to solicit reviews at all.** Its recommendation
  software also suppresses reviews it judges to be solicited. Treat Yelp as a platform to monitor
  and respond on, not to request from.
- The FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, finalized in 2024, makes fake and
  incentivized reviews a federal enforcement matter carrying civil penalties, not merely a platform
  policy problem. No short-term ranking is worth that.
- Offering a discount, a free service, or entry into a drawing in exchange for a review violates
  all of the above simultaneously.

The only sustainable strategy is doing good work and asking politely, every time.

## 7. Content-Driven Link Earning

**Effort: Medium-High · Impact: Medium-High, compounding**

Directory links plateau. Content is what earns links nobody had to ask for.

### The storm angle is the strongest asset

This is the genuinely newsworthy hook, and it is seasonal and recurring, which means it can be
worked every single year.

The verifiable facts are strong on their own: the Atlantic hurricane season runs **June 1 to
November 30**, with activity generally heaviest from mid-August into October. Texas trees and Gulf
Coast weather are a real subject with real stakes. Local news outlets, TV station websites,
insurance blogs, HOA newsletters, and municipal emergency-preparedness pages all publish storm-prep
content on a schedule, and all of them need sources.

The critical discipline: **general meteorological and horticultural facts are fair game; converting
one into a company claim is not.** "The Atlantic hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30" is a
fact. "We handle 200 storm calls a season" is invented and forbidden. The existing article
`texas-storm-season-tree-preparation.mdx` already models this correctly by attributing the season
definition to the National Hurricane Center rather than to the company.

### Working the three existing articles

| Article | Angle to push | Who links to this kind of thing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `texas-storm-season-tree-preparation.mdx`  -  *Texas Storm Season: Prepare Your Trees Before the Wind* | Pre-season preparedness. The strongest link magnet of the three. | Local news preparedness roundups, HOA and neighborhood newsletters, insurance agents, municipal emergency management pages |
| `emergency-tree-service-what-to-do.mdx`  -  *Tree Fell on Your House? What to Do in the First Hour* | Post-event safety sequence. Highly shareable during an active event. | Community Facebook groups, local news live blogs, insurance adjusters, neighborhood associations |
| `tree-pruning-guide-healthy-trees.mdx`  -  *Tree Pruning Done Right: A Texas Homeowner's Guide* | Evergreen reference. Oak wilt timing and dormant-season pruning. | Master Gardener programs, garden clubs, extension-adjacent resource pages, home improvement roundups |

Concrete moves, in order of value:

1. **Translate all three into Spanish** and publish them at their own URLs. This is the single
   highest-value content action available. Spanish-language tree care content written for Texas
   homeowners is scarce, the bilingual capability is confirmed, and Spanish-language community
   organizations and media have real audiences and almost no competition for their links. Do it
   properly: distinct URLs, `hreflang` between the pairs, translated metadata  -  not a translation
   widget.
2. **Refresh the storm article every May,** before the season starts, and update the `updated`
   frontmatter field. A current date matters when a journalist is choosing a source in August.
3. **Turn the emergency article into a one-page printable checklist**  -  the first-hour sequence and
   what to photograph. Utility resources get bookmarked and linked far more often than prose does.
4. **Pitch before the season, not during it.** In late May, send local outlets and neighborhood
   newsletters a short note offering the storm-prep material and an available bilingual expert for
   comment. Pitching during an active storm means competing with the storm.

### Content worth adding

Ranked by likely link value:

| Piece | Why it earns links | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Spanish versions of all articles** | Almost no competition; opens Spanish-language media and community organizations entirely | Medium |
| **Printable pre-storm tree checklist (PDF, both languages)** | Utility resources get linked and re-hosted by HOAs and neighborhood groups | Low |
| **"What to photograph before you touch anything"  -  documenting storm tree damage** | Insurance-adjacent and genuinely useful; earns links from preparedness content. Explain documentation only  -  make **no** claims about coverage, payouts, or the claims process | Low |
| **Texas tree care calendar  -  what to do each month** | Evergreen, endlessly referenced, seasonally re-shared | Medium |
| **Oak wilt: why spring pruning is the risk** | Real, specific, regionally important, and tied to the confirmed pruning service. Cite Texas A&M Forest Service and extension sources | Medium |
| **"How to read a tree service estimate"** | Consumer-protection framing earns links from advice sites. Explain what an estimate should itemize. **No prices, no dollar figures, no ranges** | Medium |
| **Hazard tree identification photo guide** | Uses the existing real photo library, which most competitors cannot match | Medium |
| **Storm-damaged tree: save it or remove it?** | High-intent, high-share decision content | Medium |

### The bilingual angle as a link strategy in its own right

This deserves separate billing because it is undersold. "Hablamos Español" is a confirmed fact, and
it opens channels that are effectively uncontested:

- **Spanish-language local media.** Local Univision and Telemundo affiliates run community and
  preparedness segments, and their websites publish accompanying resources. A bilingual tree expert
  available for a hurricane-season segment is a real offer, not a pitch gimmick.
- **Spanish-language community organizations, churches, and mutual aid groups**, whose resource
  pages and bulletins link out generously and are rarely targeted by competitors.
- **Hispanic chambers of commerce**, per §5.
- **Bilingual preparedness materials.** Emergency-preparedness pages actively look for Spanish
  resources to link, because they are expected or required to provide them and few exist.

None of this requires claiming a service area. It requires having genuinely useful Spanish content,
which currently does not exist and easily could.

### Journalist sourcing

Journalist-sourcing platforms connect reporters with expert sources. HARO, the long-standing one,
has been retired, and its successor was subsequently shut down as well. Platforms in this category
that operate today include **Qwoted**, **Featured**, and **SourceBottle**  -  verify each is still
operating before investing time, since this category churns.

The realistic value here is narrow: storm season, tree failure, and post-disaster safety, plus a
bilingual angle these platforms rarely have. Answer only queries that fit precisely, and never
claim a credential the business does not hold  -  respond as an experienced, insured tree service,
never as a certified arborist.

**Effort: Low per response · Impact: Low-Medium, occasionally High**

## 8. Tactics to Avoid

**Effort saved: High · Risk avoided: Very High**

Every item here is offered by someone as an SEO service. All of them are worse than doing nothing,
because doing nothing is at least recoverable.

| Tactic | What it is | Why it is a risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Buying links** | Paying a site for a link or a "guest post placement" that passes ranking signal | A direct violation of Google's link spam policy. Paid links must carry `rel="sponsored"` or `nofollow`, which removes the only reason anyone buys them. Manual actions from paid links are among the hardest to get lifted |
| **PBNs (private blog networks)** | A network of sites built solely to link to clients | Deliberate manipulation. PBNs get deindexed in batches and everything they pointed at is re-evaluated. When the network dies, the rankings go with it |
| **Mass directory blasts** | Automated submission to hundreds or thousands of directories | Near-zero link value and  -  far worse for a *local* business  -  it sprays inconsistent NAP data across the web, actively damaging the citation consistency §2 and §4 are built on. This is the tactic most likely to do real harm here |
| **Spun or bulk-generated content** | Machine-rewritten or mass-produced articles published at volume | Google's spam policies target scaled content abuse regardless of how it was produced. It also reliably produces text that eventually makes an unverifiable claim about the business  -  the exact failure mode this project exists to avoid |
| **Fake or incentivized reviews** | Buying, writing, trading, or paying for reviews; gating requests | Platform policy violations *and*, since 2024, an FTC enforcement matter carrying civil penalties. See §6 |
| **Keyword-stuffed business name** | "Romero Green Tree Service Tree Removal Stump Grinding" on the GBP listing | Violates Google's name guidelines. Competitors report it, and it is a common cause of suspension. The canonical name in §2 is the name |
| **Fake locations / virtual offices** | Creating listings at addresses the business does not occupy in order to target more cities | An explicit GBP violation, aggressively enforced. Risks suspension of the entire profile, not just the fake one |
| **Link exchanges and reciprocal link pages** | "Link to us and we'll link to you" | Excessive reciprocal linking is called out in Google's link spam policy. An occasional genuine partner link is fine; a partners page built for the exchange is not |
| **Exact-match anchor text at scale** | Every acquired link reading "emergency tree removal" | An unnatural anchor profile is a classic manipulation signal. Real links use the business name, the bare URL, or the article title |
| **"Guaranteed #1 ranking" packages** | Sold constantly to contractors, usually by cold call or text | Nobody can guarantee ranking. These packages are almost always mass directory blasts plus spun content  -  the two most damaging items on this list, bundled |
| **Claiming credentials the business does not hold** | "Licensed," "certified," "ISA certified arborist," "BBB accredited," "A+ rated" | Not merely an SEO risk. It is a false advertising exposure and in some jurisdictions a regulatory one. **The business is insured. That is the claim. It is a good claim. Use it** |

### The underlying principle

Every shortcut on this list buys a temporary ranking against a permanent liability. A tree service
does not need tricks. It needs a verified Google Business Profile, consistent NAP, genuine reviews
from real customers, a handful of real local relationships, and content that is actually useful
during hurricane season. That is the entire plan, and none of it can be penalized.
