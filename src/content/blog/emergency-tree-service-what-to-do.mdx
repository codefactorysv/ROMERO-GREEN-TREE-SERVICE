## What Should You Do in the First Hour After a Tree Falls?

In the first hour, get everyone clear of the tree and any wires, call 911 for injuries or fire, call your electric utility for anything involving lines, photograph the damage from safe ground, and then call an insured tree crew - in that order. Do not skip ahead to the cleanup.

1. **Get everyone out and away.** Move people and pets off the impact side of the house - a neighbor's place, or the far side of the property.
2. **Do a headcount outside.** Know where everyone is before you start making phone calls.
3. **Assume every downed or sagging line is live.** Stay at least 35 feet back, the clearance utilities commonly advise. That includes lines in the yard, lines in standing water, and lines draped through branches.
4. **Call 911 for injuries, a gas smell, fire, or a line down across a road.** Those are emergency-services problems first.
5. **Call your electric utility for anything involving wires.** Only the utility can de-energize a line. No responsible crew, ours included, will cut a tree resting on an energized line until the utility clears the site.
6. **Stay off the roof.** A tree strike can compromise decking and trusses you cannot see from the ground, and a wet roof is slick even where it is sound.
7. **Do not touch the tree or start a saw.** Not to "take a little weight off," not to free a car door.
8. **Photograph everything before anything moves.** More on that below.
9. **Call an insured tree crew and give them specifics.** The more precise your description, the better equipped the truck that arrives will be.

### Downed Lines Deserve Their Own Rule

Electricity does not need you to touch a wire. Current can travel out through wet ground, a chain-link fence, a metal gutter, and the tree itself - wood conducts far more readily when it is soaked with storm water. If you find yourself too close to a downed line, do not run. Shuffle away with your feet together and small steps until you are well clear.

If a line is down across your driveway and your car is under the tree, the car stays where it is. Anyone inside a vehicle that a line is touching should stay inside and wait for the utility unless there is fire.

## Why Is a Fallen Tree More Dangerous Than It Looks?

A fallen tree is more dangerous than it looks because the forces that held it upright do not disappear when it comes down. A tree that has fallen is a loaded one. Trunks bridge across a fence or a roofline and bend like a drawn bow. Limbs pinned under the canopy hold enormous stored energy. Root plates that lifted out of saturated soil can drop back into the hole and take the trunk with them.

That stored energy is called spring tension, and it is why a first cut in the wrong place can send a log rolling, snap a limb up into someone's face, or bind a saw bar solid in half a second. It is also why chainsaw injuries are a well known hazard in the days after a storm across Texas and the Gulf Coast. The saws come out, the power is off, everyone wants the driveway back, and people who have never bucked a loaded log go to work on one in the dark.

<Callout type="warning">A tree resting on a structure, a fence, a vehicle, or a line is under tension you cannot see from the ground. That is a rigging job, not a chainsaw job.</Callout>

There is a quieter risk too. A tree lying on a roof is often doing you a favor by staying put; dragging it off without support can pull sheathing, fascia, and gutters with it and turn a patch repair into a rebuild.

## What Should You Photograph Before Anything Moves?

Photograph everything before anything moves, because your insurer will ask what the damage looked like before cleanup, and once a crew makes the site safe that evidence is gone. From safe ground, get:

- **Wide shots from several angles** showing the whole tree and the whole structure it landed on.
- **The point of impact** - roof, wall, window, fence, or vehicle.
- **Interior damage**, but only if the inside is safe to enter: ceiling stains, cracks, water coming in.
- **The base of the tree**, including the root plate or break point, if you can see it from a distance.
- **Any vehicles, outbuildings, or fencing** that got hit.
- **A short video walkaround** narrating the date and what you are seeing.

Do not climb, lean out a window, or step under the canopy for a better angle. A photo is never worth it. If a crew must move something to make the site safe, that is fine - safety comes before documentation.

## What Should You Have Ready When You Call a Tree Crew?

Have ready what fell, what it landed on, whether a power line is involved, whether anyone is hurt, how a truck can reach the property, and whether the tree is still settling, because dispatch is guesswork without those details. Having these ready is the difference between the right truck showing up and a second trip:

| What we ask | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| What fell - whole tree, major limb, or uprooted trunk | Sets the size of the job and the gear |
| What it landed on - roof, car, fence, driveway, open yard | Decides whether it gets rigged down or cut in place |
| Whether any power or utility line is involved | Determines whether the utility must clear the site first |

If you are not sure about something, say so. A wrong guess costs more time than an honest "I can't see it from here."

## What an Emergency Crew Actually Does on Arrival

The first thing a crew does is not cut anything. It is read the load: where the tension is, where the compression is, and what the structure does when each piece comes off. From there:

1. **Establish a work zone.** People, vehicles, and pets out of the drop area.
2. **Confirm the utility status.** Nothing near a conductor moves until that is settled.
3. **Stabilize before removing.** Ropes, slings, and props take load off the structure so nothing shifts unexpectedly.
4. **Section under control.** Pieces come off small, rigged to a line, and lowered rather than dropped. Over a roof, every piece is a controlled descent.
5. **Relieve tension deliberately.** Bound limbs are released with the cut placed so the energy goes somewhere safe.
6. **Make it safe first, clean up second.** At two in the morning, "done" means the danger is gone and your house is not taking on more damage. Stump grinding, limb cleaning, and landscaping repairs belong to daylight.

## How Emergency Work Differs From Scheduled Work

Scheduled work is planned around the tree. Emergency work is planned around whatever the tree is currently doing to your property.

- **The load dictates the sequence, not convenience.** On a planned removal you choose where the pieces fall. On an emergency you take what the tension allows.
- **Conditions are worse.** Wind, rain, mud, darkness, no power. Everything takes longer and needs more eyes on it.
- **It often takes more than one visit.** Make the site safe now, then handle full cleanup and follow-up work like [stump grinding](/#stump-grinding), limb cleaning, or mulching once the ground dries out. You can see finished results in [our work](/#our-work).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does homeowners insurance cover emergency tree removal?

Emergency tree removal is often covered when the fallen tree damaged a covered structure such as a roof, garage, or fence, but coverage is decided by your carrier under your specific policy terms and most policies cap what they pay toward removal. A tree that came down in open yard with no structure damage is frequently treated differently. Notify your insurer as soon as everyone is safe, keep every invoice and work order, and ask what the policy requires before authorizing anything beyond making the site safe.

### What does emergency tree removal cost after a storm?

Emergency tree removal is priced job by job, and the biggest factors are the size of the tree, what it landed on, whether the wood has to be rigged and lowered instead of dropped, and how close a truck can get to it. Work over a roof or near a line takes more equipment and time than a trunk lying in an open yard. ### Can a tree on my roof wait until morning?

No, a tree resting on a roof should be assessed right away, because the load can shift as wind, rain, and the tree's own weight work on decking and trusses you cannot see from the ground. Every hour it sits, more water gets into the opening it made. ### What should I do if a tree is leaning but has not fallen yet?

Treat a shifted tree as an active hazard: keep people, pets, and vehicles out of a circle wider than the tree is tall, and do not try to rope, stake, or push it back upright. Fresh cracks in the soil, a root plate lifting on one side, or a lean that changed after a storm all mean the roots are already failing, and saturated ground plus another gust can finish it without warning.
