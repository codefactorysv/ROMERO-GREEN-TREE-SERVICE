Good pruning is one of the best things you can do for a tree, and bad pruning is one of the most damaging. The difference isn't the saw - it's knowing which branches to remove, exactly where to cut, and what time of year to do it. Here's how to get all three right.

## Trimming and Pruning Are Not the Same Job

People use the two words interchangeably, but they solve different problems:

**Trimming** is about shape and clearance - lifting limbs off the roof, pulling growth back from the driveway, opening a sight line. The goal is how the tree fits the space around it.

**Pruning** is about health and structure - taking out what's dead or diseased, correcting a weak branch union before it fails, improving how the canopy handles wind. The goal is what the tree looks like in ten years, not next weekend.

A good crew does both in one visit but never confuses the two - trimming for clearance without thinking about structure is how a healthy tree ends up lopsided. Both are part of our [tree services](/#services).

## The Four Cuts That Matter

Almost every worthwhile cut falls into one of four categories.

### 1. Deadwood Removal

Dead limbs don't come back, and they don't stay put. They dry out, get brittle, and eventually drop - usually in wind, usually onto whatever is below. Removing deadwood costs the tree nothing in stored energy, so it works in any season.

### 2. Crown Cleaning

Deadwood plus the other obvious problems: broken stubs from an old storm, diseased limbs, crossing branches that rub each other raw, and water sprouts shooting up from old cuts. It's the standard maintenance prune, and it's all most mature trees need.

### 3. Crown Thinning, Done Sparingly

Thinning means selectively removing a few live interior branches so light and air move through the canopy. Done lightly, it can ease the sail effect that catches wind. Done heavily, it backfires: you strip out the growth that feeds the tree, force weakly attached sprouts, and end up with a thin, whippy canopy instead of a stronger one.

### 4. Structural Cuts on Young Trees

This is the pruning almost nobody does, and it has the biggest payoff. On a young tree you want one dominant central leader, branches spaced along the trunk rather than clustered, and no codominant stems - two stems of roughly equal size splitting off with a tight V-shaped crotch. That union can trap bark inside the joint and become a classic failure point twenty years later.

The fix is usually a *subordination cut*: instead of removing the competing stem, you shorten it so the leader you want outgrows it. A few minutes with hand pruners now prevents a much bigger problem later.

## Where the Cut Goes: The Branch Collar

Trees don't heal wounds the way people do - they can't replace damaged tissue, so they wall it off and grow around it. That job depends on the **branch collar**, the slightly swollen ring of trunk tissue at the base of every branch.

A proper cut goes just *outside* the collar, leaving it intact. The tree then grows callus in from the edges and closes the wound over time. Two cuts break that process:

- **Flush cuts** slice into the collar, level with the trunk. They look neat, and they're the worse of the two: you've removed the tissue that closes the wound and opened the trunk to decay.
- **Stub cuts** leave inches of branch past the collar. The stub can't grow new tissue, so it dies back and decay works inward toward the trunk.

For any limb heavy enough to tear, use the three-cut method:

1. Cut upward from the underside about a foot out from the collar, a third of the way through.
2. Cut down from the top an inch or two farther out, so the limb drops without stripping bark down the trunk.
3. Make the final cut just outside the collar, taking off the stub cleanly.

Skip step one and a falling limb can peel bark several feet down the trunk - a wound far worse than the pruning itself.

## The 25 Percent Rule

Leaves are how a tree makes food. Take too many at once and you starve it - the result is stress, dieback, and weak sprout growth. The common working limit is **no more than about 25 percent of the live canopy in one growing season** - and most well-kept trees need far less than that.

| Tree situation | Live canopy to remove in one season |
| --- | --- |
| Young tree being trained for structure | Up to about 25 percent |
| Healthy mature shade tree | 10 to 15 percent is usually plenty |
| Older, stressed, or storm-damaged tree | Deadwood only, or very little live wood |

Dead limbs don't count toward that limit - you can take out every dead branch without touching the tree's food supply.

## Topping and Lion-Tailing: The Two Worst Things You Can Do

**Topping** is cutting main limbs back to stubs to make a tree shorter. It doesn't make a tree safer - it makes it more dangerous. The tree answers with dozens of fast sprouts anchored only in the outer layer of wood, while the stub cuts never close and decay works down into the limb. A few years later you have heavy new growth standing on rotting wood. If a tree really is too big for its spot, the honest options are reduction cuts back to a suitable lateral branch, or removal.

**Lion-tailing** is stripping out interior growth so each limb ends in a puff of leaves at the tip. It's often sold as thinning, and it's the opposite of helpful: the weight shifts to the branch tips, the interior growth that fed and tapered the limb is gone, and what's left is a lever waiting for a gust. ## Timing: When to Prune Along the Texas Coast

### Dormant Season Is the Default

For most shade trees, late winter dormancy is the sweet spot: the leaves are off so structure is easy to read, pests and pathogens are far less active, and the tree pushes new growth right after the cut. Winter work also puts the job well ahead of hurricane season - June 1 through November 30, as the National Hurricane Center defines it.

### Oaks Need Extra Care

Oak wilt is a fungal disease that kills oaks, and it spreads two ways: through connected root systems, and by sap-feeding beetles carrying spores to a fresh wound. Those beetles are most active in spring, so the standard guidance across Texas is to avoid pruning oaks in the high-risk window - roughly February through June - and to save the work for the cold of winter or the heat of midsummer.

<Callout type="warning">If an oak is cut or wounded at any time of year, paint the wound right away - within minutes, not hours. Oaks are the main exception to the general rule that wound dressing does not help a tree.</Callout>

### Dead and Hazardous Limbs: Any Season

There's no wrong season to remove a dead, cracked, or hanging limb. A branch over a bedroom or driveway is a safety issue, not a horticultural one - it comes out now. Same for anything left hanging after a storm - here's [what to do when a tree comes down](/blog/emergency-tree-service-what-to-do).

## When It Stops Being a Ladder Job

Hand pruners and a pole saw are fine for small limbs you can reach with both feet on the ground. The job changes when any of this is true:

- The cut is above head height and you'd need a ladder to reach it
- The limb is heavy enough that where it lands is a decision, not an accident
- Something valuable is underneath - a roof, fence, car, pool, or neighbor's yard
- Power lines run anywhere near the work
- The limb is dead, cracked, or partly hung up, so it may move unpredictably

Ladders and chainsaws are a bad combination: a ladder needs the limb to stay still, and a limb under saw pressure rarely does. Work like that is done from inside the tree instead - a climber on rope can get into position for a clean cut outside the collar, and rigging lowers heavy pieces under control.
